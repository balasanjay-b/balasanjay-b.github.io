Project 01 · Summer 2026

The Batmobile: an autonomous competition rover

1st place Fastest rover to complete the full course

A mecanum-wheeled rover built by a student team at UCLA Summer Sessions. It drives itself through an unknown obstacle course using four ultrasonic sensors. I designed the chassis and the sensor mounts in Onshape and took both from first sketch to fabricated part.

ProgramUCLA Summer Sessions, Engineering Design: Rovers (Session A)
My roleCAD lead for chassis and sensor holders
TeamTeam project, "The Vigilantes"
CADOnshape part studios and full assembly
FabricationLaser-cut acrylic, FDM 3D printing
ElectronicsArduino Mega, 2 motor drivers, 4 HC-SR04 sensors
The challenge

Build a rover that can find its own way

The course asked each team to design, wire, and program a microcontroller-driven rover. The final competition was an unknown obstacle course: rovers had to navigate it on their own and react to walls and obstacles, followed by a team presentation.

Our team set three goals for the build: make room on the chassis for every component, give the rover a look nobody would mistake for another team's, and above all get the whole system running.

Chassis

Four iterations to a chassis that fits everything

Our theme was Batman, so the first chassis took its outline from the bat symbol. Each revision balanced that look against a practical problem: the Arduino Mega, a breadboard, two motor drivers, a battery, and a front claw all needed a place to mount. The side notches clear the mecanum wheels, and a grid of mounting holes let us place and re-place components without recutting the plate.

Envelope12 × 10 in
Thickness≈ 3.5 mm acrylic
Corner filletsR15 / R20 mm
ProcessLaser cut from DXF
Drive4 mecanum wheels
Revisions4
Sensor holder

Trading the bat ears for a better fit

Each HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor needs a mount that holds its two transducers square to the direction it measures. My first 3D-printed holder had tall bat ears to match the theme. They were bulky and stood too high, and when the team moved the sensors underneath the chassis to free up space on top, the ears no longer worked. I stripped the design down to a low-profile bracket that tucks under the plate.

→
CAD to build

Plan it in the assembly, then cut once

Before cutting any acrylic, I placed the Arduino Mega, breadboard, motor drivers, sensors, and claw in an Onshape assembly to check spacing and hole alignment. The built rover matched the CAD layout.

Result
1st

First place in the final competition. The Batmobile was the fastest rover to navigate the full obstacle course and reach the end.

What I learned

Takeaways

  • Function sets the outline. Looks matter, but the chassis only came together once every component had a planned place to mount.
  • Iterate cheaply. Four chassis revisions and two sensor holders were fast to try because the design lived in CAD first.
  • Design for the wiring. Loose jumper wires kept unplugging during testing. Next time I'd add cable routing and strain relief to the chassis instead of fixing it with tape.
  • Understand the code you run. Our team leaned on AI tools to debug the Arduino code, and learned that you can only fix what you understand.
Project 02 · Fall 2026

Pollen filter: a two-ramp sorting system

Design complete FIRST Tech Challenge, Team 547

A passive sorter for my school's FTC robot. Game pieces called pollen come in two sizes, and the robot has to separate them. My design uses two stacked ramps: large pollen rides across the top ramp, and small pollen drops through a gap in it onto a lower ramp that sends it the other way.

ProgramFIRST Tech Challenge, 2026–27 season
TeamTeam 547, Gretchen Whitney High School
My roleDesigned the filter mechanism in CAD
CADOnshape: 3 part studios and an assembly
PartsBase, top ramp, bottom ramp
Envelope10 × 5 × 6.8 in
The challenge

Two sizes of pollen, two different destinations

In this season's game, the robot collects balls called pollen that come in a larger and a smaller size, and each size needs to go to a different place. Sorting them by hand-off or by a sensor-driven gate would add motors, code, and things that can fail during a match. I wanted the pieces to sort themselves by size, using only gravity and the geometry of the part.

How it works

Let the gap do the sorting

  1. Pollen enters at the high end of the top ramp, an open frame made of two side rails with a gap between them.
  2. Large pollen is wider than the gap, so it rests on both rails and rolls down the top ramp to one exit.
  3. Small pollen fits between the rails and falls through onto the bottom ramp.
  4. The bottom ramp slopes the opposite way, sending small pollen to the other side of the robot.
Parts

Three parts, designed to fit together

I modeled each part in its own part studio and combined them in an assembly to check clearances. The ramps pin into holes in the base's side walls, and the base has fillets and chamfers where the walls meet the floor for added strength.

Result
Not used

The team chose a faster approach for the final robot. After reviewing the design, we decided that a gravity-fed sorter would be too slow for match play, where every second of cycle time counts. My two-ramp filter stayed a finished design that never went onto the robot.

What I learned

Takeaways

  • Simple isn't always the best choice. I went with gravity because it needed no motors or code. That made it reliable, but it also capped how fast pieces could move through it.
  • Set the requirements first. Next time I'd pin down a target cycle time before designing, so speed is part of the brief from the start and not something discovered at review.
  • Test early with real parts. A quick printed prototype and a stopwatch would have shown the speed problem sooner and made the team's decision easier.
  • A design that isn't used can still be useful. Working through this one helped the team compare options and settle on what the final robot needed.
